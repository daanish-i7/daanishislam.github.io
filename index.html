Bed Sensor Flange Mount & FANUC Robot Programming

Redwood Materials · Reno, NV

Designed and assembled a custom fixture to spin a bed sensor flange for a thermal spray coating operation. Engineered around real constraints, targeting up to 250 RPM while staying within motor torque limits, selecting 11-gauge mild steel for the rotating section and 2×2 square steel tubing for the fixed stand. Built in tab-and-slot features to simplify welding and assembly, and ran RPM and robot speed calculations across different flange diameters to dial in process parameters.

Part of a broader HVOF thermal spray operation that improved coating cycle time by ~12%.

Zipline P2 Delivery Droid — Modelling & FEA

Georgia Institute of Technology · ME 4042 · Atlanta, GA

Modelled and analyzed the Zipline P2 delivery droid — the small autonomous sub-vehicle that descends from the main drone to achieve doorstep-level delivery accuracy in urban environments. The droid uses three motors and propellers to counteract wind loads and precisely position itself during descent, and the goal of the project was to accurately replicate its geometry in Siemens NX and perform computational and structural analysis to validate the design against key performance specifications.

The main body was built from scratch by importing six-sided raster reference images into NX and scaling them to a known bounding box. Bounding curves were generated for half the body and surfaced using Through Curves, Through Curve Meshes, and Studio Surfaces. The resulting half-body sheet was mirrored about the symmetry plane, and all surfaces were sewn into a closed solid. Edge Blend operations were applied along all surface seams to achieve smooth curvature transitions. Following the smooth body creation, Sweep and Extrude subtract operations were used to cut openings for the side propeller assemblies, rear motor bay, light bars, drone feet, front screen, lid, food/medicine compartment, and bay doors — with extruded hinge features added to interface with the lid mechanism.

Full Assembly — Closed Configuration
Full Assembly — Open Lid Configuration
Transparent View — Internal Motor & Bay Door Mechanism

The main propeller sub-assembly required the highest modeling complexity in the project. Each blade was defined by sketching cross-sectional airfoil profiles on a series of offset datum planes along the blade span, capturing the varying chord width and twist angle at each station. A Through-Curves loft was applied through all cross-sectional sketches to generate a smooth, twisted blade surface. A single blade was then patterned in a five-instance circular array about the hub axis. The hub itself was modeled as a cylindrical boss with a tapered bore to receive the motor shaft, united with a short collar extrusion. The propeller cover — a cage-like annular guard — was modeled as a circular extrude with a concentric cutout, with radial structural bars patterned circumferentially from a central hub ring to the outer rim. The mounting bracket was modeled as an L-shaped extrusion with edge blends at the internal corner to reduce stress concentration, with bolt patterns on both faces for attachment to the main body and motor rear face.

Main Propeller Sub-Assembly — Twisted Blade Geometry

The bay door mechanism translates rotational motor output into simultaneous lateral displacement of both delivery bay door panels. The door panels were derived directly from the main body solid using an Extrude subtraction, ensuring geometric consistency between the panel outer faces and the body opening with no fit tolerance issues. Integral rack features were extruded from the inner face of each panel, with trapezoidal gear teeth applied as a patterned extrude cut. A pinion gear was modeled with a circular tooth array and a central through-hole to receive the motor shaft. In the sub-assembly, revolute and translational joints were defined at the shaft axis and panel sliding axes respectively, and a rack-and-pinion motion coupling was applied to enforce equal and opposite panel displacement for a given pinion rotation angle.

A static structural FEA was performed on the cross-shaped motor mount using Simcenter NX with the Nastran SOL 101 Linear Statics solver. The geometry was meshed with CTETRA(10) 10-node quadratic tetrahedral elements at a global size of 1mm, with local refinement at bolt holes and fillet transitions. Both arm end faces were fully constrained to replicate bolted attachment to the main body, and a 5N uniform pressure was applied to the central motor pocket face in the negative Z direction — consistent with gravitational and dynamic thrust loading. The baseline Aluminum 6061 result at 0.25 inch thickness yielded a maximum von Mises stress of 0.589 MPa against a yield strength of 270 MPa, giving a safety factor of approximately 458. Maximum nodal displacement was 9.412×10⁻⁴ mm, confirming the part operates fully within the elastic regime with negligible deformation. A parametric study was then conducted varying material (Aluminum 6061, Nylon, Titanium Ti-6Al-4V) and arm thickness (0.125, 0.25, 0.375 inches), with results showing that stress is geometry-driven and thickness-dependent, while material selection is governed by stiffness and weight rather than structural necessity.

LCST Desalination Loop — Mass Exchanger Design

Water–Energy Research Lab · Georgia Tech

Designed and 3D printed mass exchanger components for a Single Stage LCST (Lower Critical Solution Temperature) desalination loop — a thermally-driven process that separates water from brine using a polymer solution that phase-shifts at a critical temperature. The system cycles brine through warm solution (WS) and water recovery (WR) stages, with air pumps driving humid air across mass exchanger beds while humidity (RH), temperature (T), and flow (F) sensors monitor conditions at each node.

Single Stage LCST Process Diagram — Water–Energy Research Lab, Georgia Tech

Using SolidWorks, designed detailed CAD models of the mass exchanger components and integrated mounting features for humidity and pressure sensors, control valves, a motor, and a water pump. All components were 3D printed in-house and assembled into a functional test loop. Used Sensirion sensors for real-time humidity and temperature data acquisition throughout testing.

SolidWorks CAD — Mass Exchanger Components
Physical Test Setup — Assembled Loop

Built and tested multiple configurations of the mass exchanger to evaluate desalination performance, mass exchange efficiency, and leakage control under varying conditions. Each configuration informed design iterations, adjusting geometry, seal interfaces, and flow paths, to progressively improve system performance.

Live System — Flow Test

The final configuration achieved a relative humidity increase from 25.7% to 96.7%, validating the mass exchanger's ability to drive effective moisture transfer across the loop.

Classic VW Beetle — Surface Modeling in Siemens NX

Georgia Institute of Technology · ME 4042

Designed a full 3D model of a classic Volkswagen Beetle in Siemens NX, starting from scratch using spline-based wireframe construction and building up to a complete solid body assembly. The project required generating all geometry from parametric curves — no imported bodies — forcing every surface patch, blend, and transition to be explicitly defined and modifiable.

Spline Wireframe & Curves — Body Profile Construction
Final Rendered Model — Fully Surfaced Solid Body

Surfaces were generated from the spline network using N-Sided Surface, Through Curve Mesh, Ruled, and Fill Surface operations. Through Curve Mesh was critical for the roof and hood panels; by defining primary and cross curves simultaneously, curvature-continuous G2 surface patches were achieved across all major body sections. Interior curves were used within UV orientation settings to control surface shape without introducing additional boundary constraints.

Achieving G2 continuity across the hood, roof, and door panels was a core requirement, enforced by carefully matching tangent direction and curvature magnitude at all shared edges between adjacent patches. Edge Blend operations were applied throughout to eliminate sharp transitions, with individual segment blending used at complex corners where chain blending caused failures. Additional features including bumpers, headlights, door trim lines, window frames, wheel hubs, and axles were modeled beyond the basic requirements.

Tethered UAV Relay System — Better Place Drones

Georgia Institute of Technology · VIP Program · Atlanta, GA

Contributing to the Tethered Relay Development subteam of the Better Place Drones VIP program, focused on enabling persistent aerial communication relay using a tethered X8 octocopter UAV. The tethered configuration eliminates battery endurance constraints by supplying continuous power via a grounded tether, allowing indefinite hover time for relay operations. Work spans from system-level component procurement research through mechanical design and fabrication of custom payload mounting assemblies.

Conducted a structured market survey of commercially available tethered power systems to identify viable off-the-shelf solutions for the X8 platform. Evaluated systems across key parameters including converter requirement, input voltage range, continuous output wattage, tether height and cable type, cable weight per unit length, UI/control interface, and unit cost. Systems assessed included the Fotokite T3500-S, Maverick C2J TK3, Volarous V-LINE PRO, ELISTAIR LIGH-T4, Zenith Aero Tech, USAS, Fotokite Sigma, Hoverfly, Mistral Kite, RS3000 Ground Power System, and SkyCoach Drone-Tek — spanning converter topologies from 48VDC/2500W to vehicle-based power systems, and tether lengths from 45m to 400ft. Key selection criteria included compatibility with 48VDC/2500W input requirements, tether height above 400ft (122m), auto-winch capability, and cable weight below 500g for flight budget compliance.

Tether Kit Procurement Research — Comparative System Analysis Matrix

Designed and modeled two iterations of a battery and payload mount assembly for the X8 UAV in SolidWorks, sized to house onboard fuel tanks, electronics bays, and structural attachment interfaces. The first configuration is a rectilinear carbon fiber composite enclosure — an open-frame box structure with internal vertical ribs for component compartmentalization, mounted on dual lateral threaded rod rails with nut-and-clamp fastening for positional adjustability along the airframe. Panel geometry was designed for sheet material fabrication with bolted corner extrusions.

The second configuration is a cantilevered asymmetric mount integrating two cylindrical pressure/fuel tanks — one forward-mounted at an angled orientation and one vertically suspended below the frame — connected via carbon fiber tube struts with threaded rod terminal fittings. The irregular geometry was intentionally designed to offset the center of mass for balance correction relative to the UAV's existing payload distribution. Both designs account for mass budget constraints, structural rigidity under flight loads, and accessibility for field servicing.

Drug Delivery Drone — Concept & System Design

Georgia Institute of Technology · Atlanta, GA

Developed a full concept and system design for a real-world application: an autonomous drug delivery drone. The design objective was to produce a complete, manufacturable assembly within a fixed timeline — from individual components through to a final integrated model.

The drone follows a symmetric X-frame layout with four arms extending from a central hub. Each arm terminates in a propeller guard modeled using swept and lofted features in SolidWorks to produce the curved guard geometry. The propellers themselves were modeled as separate sub-assemblies with individual blade geometry defined using surface modeling tools.

The central body houses a dedicated electronics bay with rectangular cutouts for battery and control board mounting, modeled using extruded cuts and shell features to minimize weight while maintaining structural integrity. A payload compartment hangs below the main frame, designed to securely carry a medical package — built using SolidWorks weldments and sheet metal features. Landing gear legs extend from the lower frame, modeled as structural members with filleted joints.

All parts were designed individually and brought together using SolidWorks assembly mates — coincident, concentric, and distance mates — to fully constrain the model. Creo was used for advanced modeling and final presentation visuals.

ME 2110 — Programmable Robot Design & Build

Georgia Institute of Technology · Atlanta, GA

Designed and built a fully programmable robot for a competitive design challenge, integrating DC motors, pulleys, pneumatic pistons, solenoids, encoders, and sensors into a cohesive electromechanical system.
